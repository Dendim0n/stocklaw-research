## Core Framework

The author decomposes stock price into two parts:
- **Current Value** = EPS × 10 (fair PE assuming zero growth)
- **Future Value** = Market Price - Current Value (premium for growth expectations)

Higher "future value" ratio means more dependence on future growth, hence more uncertainty.

## Data Verification

Magnificent 7 future value ratios (article ~5/8 vs current):

| Company | Article FV% | Current FV% | Change |
|---------|------------|-------------|--------|
| TSLA | 95.5% | 94.1% | -1.4% |
| NVDA | 66.7% | 43.3% | -23.4% (EPS revised from $7.20 to $12.63) |
| AMZN | 71.4% | 61.5% | -9.9% |
| AAPL | 69.7% | 69.5% | -0.2% |
| MSFT | 50.6% | 56.2% | +5.6% |
| META | 43.0% | 39.5% | -3.5% |
| GOOG | 71.0% | 59.6% | -11.4% |

**Key finding:** Within a month, NVDA and GOOG's FV% dropped significantly because analysts revised earnings upward. The "66.8% average" is a static snapshot that's already improving.

## Scenario Analysis

S&P 500 at 7610 vs article's base of 7150 (+6.4%). All math verified correct, but the "no upside at 22x" claim was proven wrong by the market — Forward EPS revised from $325 to ~$369.

## Score: 8.5/10

Original framework, professional scenario matrix. Core weakness: FV% is highly dependent on analyst estimates and changes dynamically. The "danger signal" could be falsified if companies continue beating expectations.
