## 3. Assessment and counter-evidence

**A. This is a policy discount, but a purely political explanation misses half of it.**
The case for policy is the structure: the worst damage was in **memory and equipment** (Micron −17.46%, AMD −17.26%, SOX −12.25%) while the hyperscalers fell only modestly (Microsoft −2.5%, Amazon −1.5%, Jin10 00:58); and the credit market stayed open for non-AI exposure the same day (Apple 4x covered, tighter spreads than peers).
But the **counter-evidence is just as hard**: Jin10's 234483 piece states that **not one of the S&P 500's eleven sectors closed higher**, and selling spread from the supply chain to the whole market. A single "data-centre permitting" variable cannot explain that; it looks more like a joint position and liquidity squeeze. Jin10 cites Vanda Analytics as saying retail net selling on Thursday may be the highest single day since the pandemic, and FedWatch Advisors as saying mechanical/program selling amplified the move — **both are second-hand, I did not obtain the underlying statistics, so they support direction only and cannot be used as evidence of "who sold"**.
**Conclusion**: the policy discount is real (it explains the **distribution** of losses) but not the **breadth**. Blaming the whole decline on "Democrats will regulate AI" is today's easiest attribution error. **Falsifier**: if no hyperscaler cuts its 2026/27 capex guidance within two weeks, the "permitting slowdown → capex cut" chain is unproven and this looks more like a valuation and positioning event.

**B. Asia's amplification is catch-up plus leverage, not new information.**
Evidence: Korea opened −2.24%, reached −8% and tripped the breaker by 08:04, covering a 6.65-point range in forty minutes (from −2.13% at the high to −8.78% at the low), with losses concentrated entirely in memory (SK Hynix −14%, Samsung −11%); Japan's damage was in equipment and SoftBank (−13%/−14%); and **there was no new Asian fundamental news** — Japanese wage data actually beat expectations. **Counter-evidence**: Nasdaq futures were already +0.86% at 08:39 and US after-hours memory names were +4%, so the marginal US selling pressure had faded after 05:00. **Implication**: Korea and Japan are **lagging variables**, not leading indicators; using a KOSPI circuit breaker to forecast another down night in the US does not hold up. **Falsifier**: if KOSPI closes below 4,911.28 and the Nikkei below 56,971, Asian selling is independent of the US and this section must be downgraded.

**C. Crude's 3.9% is a war-premium refill, not stronger demand.**
Evidence: what rallied was the **front months** (Brent Jan +3.88%, WTI Dec +2.72%), on the Hormuz attack and the "diplomacy will not be quick" headlines; meanwhile the **physical market was cutting prices** (Aramco's December Asia OSP widened to a $7 discount) and Canada's budget still assumes $87 WTI for 2026. **Counter-evidence**: Brent had just settled −8.67% the day before, so the two-day total is still a net decline; and at 07:34 Jin10 quoted East Ocean Futures arguing that "further downside in oil is limited" and that "about $20/bbl of geopolitical premium is rapidly fading" — that is a **sell-side view, not a fact**. **Conclusion**: Brent at 98.29 sits between premium refill and loose physicals, with no direction; yesterday's de-escalation trade has been interrupted but one day is not a reversal. **Falsifier**: if the Iranian proposal reaches actual text, the premium gets unwound again; if Hormuz sees a **verifiable loading disruption**, 98.29 is only the start.

**D. Rates: the short end is speaking for equities; the long end is not following.**
Evidence: 2Y −12bp, 5Y −10bp, 10Y −6bp, 30Y −3bp (CMT), with 2s10s steepening from +32bp to +38bp. That is a growth/policy-expectations curve shape, not a flight to duration. Combined with CME's December odds going 81.5%→68.7%, the mechanism is clear: equities crash → financial conditions tighten → the market marks its own hike odds down. **Counter-evidence**: Barkin and Hammack both argued the same day that "gradual hikes remain reasonable" and that policy "needs to become restrictive"; and today's 21:30 payrolls print is the direct test (expected +65k vs +29k prior, unemployment expected 4.2%). **Conclusion**: 68.7% is still a likely hike — reading it as "the hiking cycle is over" is wrong; the real information is that the market pushed the **timing** out, not that it cancelled the move. **Falsifier**: if payrolls beat 65k materially with hourly earnings ≥0.3% m/m, hike odds should rebound and the short-end rally reverses.

**E. Gold did not deliver a haven bid, and that is the most informative thing about it today.**
Evidence: spot settled 4,089.12 on Nov 5 (+0.72%) and trades 4,100.73 (+0.28%) this morning, with a session low of 4,071.15. **On the same night the Nasdaq fell 5.52%, KOSPI broke a circuit breaker and VIX rose 69.66% to 28.18, a 98.8th-percentile reading over 260 sessions — yet gold added just 0.28%.** SPDR holdings were flat, and the WGC's 321t of Q3 central-bank buying is a **quarterly stock narrative** that cannot explain an intraday move. At 05:39 Goldman cut its end-2026 target by $200 to 4,150, explicitly citing "the effect on positioning of the Fed's recent hawkish shift". **Counter-evidence**: ANZ at 08:24 attributed the rise to expectations that a Democrat-led Congress will fight inflation more effectively — a **hopeful reading of policy**, and the mirror image of the same political fact being used to explain the equity crash. **The same fact cannot explain both the crash and the gold bid; that means it is not the explanation.** **Conclusion**: gold is trading off real rates and positioning, not off fear; calling 4,100 a haven confirmation is wrong, and calling it the start of a trend lacks evidence.
