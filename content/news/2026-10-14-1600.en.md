## Key Increments

### 1. Houthi strike hits Saudi Aramco's Dhahran campus, yet crude falls back to the intraday low

- **Time**: 14 Oct, 15:24 CST｜**Sources**: Jin10 flash; Reuters, AFP and Bloomberg reporting in parallel (Saudi Energy Ministry statement and a Reuters company source)
- **Content**: Houthi forces fired missiles and drones at multiple Saudi cities; an administrative tower in Saudi Aramco's eastern Dhahran campus was hit by a ballistic missile, damaging the top three floors and starting a fire. The Energy Ministry described a "limited fire, no casualties". The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted multiple incoming targets over Riyadh, Dhahran and the south, and that debris from two intercepted cruise missiles wounded four people in Riyadh. In parallel, Iran stepped up attacks on ships transiting the Strait of Hormuz. On price, the platform WTI quote is $93.202, down 0.73% on the day, sitting at the bottom of today's range.
- **Impact**: **This is the hour's most important price-versus-narrative divergence.** The building hit was administrative, and there is no evidence of damaged loading capacity or production — so "supply disruption" pricing has not followed. Treating an escalation of attacks as automatically bullish for crude is wrong. Relatively bullish for tanker and freight-related segments; bearish for cost-sensitive refining.

### 2. Standard Chartered: Hormuz transport is far from normal; Goldman: average crude freight rose from $4 to $30 a barrel in a year

- **Time**: 14 Oct 16:00 CST (Standard Chartered) / Goldman analysis dated 10-13, summarised by Jin10 at 15:19｜**Sources**: Jin10 news ID 232440; Goldman Sachs estimates
- **Content**: Standard Chartered estimates September Gulf crude and condensate exports at about 16.5 million b/d, close to pre-conflict levels, but the share shipped through the Strait of Hormuz has fallen to roughly 60% from about 83% before the conflict; nearly 40% of flows now move via costlier, less efficient routes such as diversions and ship-to-ship transfers. Goldman Sachs estimates average global crude freight has climbed to about $30/bbl from about $4 a year ago, with Middle East routes at about $35/bbl, and the Murban-Basrah spread widening to $43 from $2 in February.
- **Impact**: **What determines crude's medium-term direction is transport cost and inventory rebuilding, not the number of attacks.** Recovering export volume masks rising transport cost, which explains both why crude has become insensitive to attacks and why regional spreads keep widening. Relatively bullish for tankers, freight-related plays and cross-region spread trades; refining feedstock costs under pressure.

### 3. AI capex gets two independent supports, diverging from the selloff in Chinese AI names

- **Time**: 14 Oct, 15:09 and 15:05 CST｜**Sources**: Jin10 flash (TrendForce AI Server research; JPMorgan research note on ASML's Q3)
- **Content**: TrendForce expects the top nine global CSPs to spend more than $957bn on capex in 2026, up 106% year on year, and to exceed $1.5tn in 2027, up 60% — both above previous expectations. JPMorgan noted ASML's Q3 gross margin of 57.5% came in above the near-56% consensus and above the top of the company's 55%–57% guidance range, driven mainly by maintenance and upgrade revenue on installed tools; Q4 guidance of about 57% is roughly 60bp above the 56.4% consensus. Counter-evidence: Broadcom CEO Hock Tan (10-12) said that with rates rising, access to capital could become one of the biggest constraints for AI companies; Omdia showed the same day that mainland China handset shipments fell 12% year on year to 59.3 million units in Q3, the lowest since 2013, with high memory costs suppressing demand.
- **Impact**: **Recurring, high-margin maintenance and upgrade revenue is more durable than order visibility — relatively bullish for semiconductor equipment and the memory chain; but memory inflation is now feeding into end demand, which is bearish for consumer-electronics OEMs.** Today A-share CPO and semiconductor names led declines, showing that industry positives do not automatically translate into these names' prices.

## Situation Assessment

**View 1: Crude should not be chased on "escalation" — short-term bias is neutral-to-bearish, with the driver shifting from supply volume to transport cost and inventory rebuilding.** Evidence: the Dhahran campus was hit by a missile but suffered only a limited office-tower fire with no casualties, and there is no sign of damaged loading or production capacity; Iraq's oil ministry said at 14:50 that exports had recovered to about 2.7 million b/d; OPEC's 10-13 monthly report cut 2026 global demand growth to 180,000 b/d from 380,000 previously, a sixth consecutive downgrade, while raising its 2027 demand growth forecast on post-war inventory rebuilding. Together these show there is no quantified "supply disruption" evidence, whereas there is evidence that high prices are curbing consumption. Asset implications: crude range-bound to soft, tankers/freight and cross-region spread trades relatively favoured, refining and airlines cost-pressured. Confirmation requires actual loading disruptions, another jump in insurance or freight, or proof that Iran's export collapse is a lasting state; invalidation: if attacks shift to export terminals or Aramco operating centres, the supply-volume narrative returns immediately and crude can jump.

**View 2: AI hardware fundamentals are strengthening, but short-term prices are driven by risk appetite — do not chase, watch first.** Evidence: TrendForce now sees 2027 CSP capex above $1.5tn, and JPMorgan attributes ASML's margin beat to recurring, high-margin maintenance and upgrade revenue; data released by China's customs administration at 10:05 today shows integrated-circuit export value up 108.1% year on year for January–September, indicating physical AI-electronics demand is intact. Counter-evidence is equally clear: Broadcom's CEO publicly warned that access to capital may be AI companies' biggest constraint; today A-share CPO, semiconductor and solar names led declines with STAR 50 down 3.29%, and the Hang Seng closed 0.26% lower. My trade-off: rank valuation elasticity behind cash quality, do not chase at this level, and wait for CPI tonight and the post-open US structure before acting. Asset implications: semiconductor equipment, memory and compute hardware relatively favoured; consumer-electronics OEMs pressured by memory inflation. What is missing: updates this week on actual CSP capex and financing structures, and whether the A-share AI decline had foreign or margin-financing participation — neither is available this hour, so this view is weaker than the first.

**View 3: Ahead of CPI, gold is "waiting for data", not "waiting for direction", and lacks a short-term push.** Evidence: platform spot gold at $4,149.45, down 0.27% on the day, with today's range only $4,143.10–4,170.39; SGE gold T+D closed down 0.67% and silver T+D down 2.9%, showing domestic desks reducing; what institutions are bullish on is Treasury duration return (two independent statements — BlackRock at 13:41 and DWS at 15:09), not gold. My bias is not to chase gold before CPI: $4,143 and $4,170 are today's measured range boundaries and serve only as observation references, not breakout thresholds. If core comes in at 0.3% or above, higher real yields would pressure risk assets first, raising gold's relative allocation value; if core is 0.2% or below, gold loses its short-term driver while growth stocks benefit. Invalidation: if CPI and geopolitics deteriorate together (core above consensus plus actual loading disruption in Hormuz), gold and crude would rise together, and the "Treasuries find buyers" allocation logic would need re-evaluating.
